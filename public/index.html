Where is inflation going?

Reported US inflation compares today's prices with prices 12 months ago, so it can move even when the pace of price rises doesn't. This page rebuilds the official CPI from its parts using live data, runs it forward, and sets the result next to what markets and professional forecasters expect.

      What markets and forecasters expect

      Fast-moving outside reads to check the model against. These sources measure slightly different things, so compare them by direction and rough level rather than to the decimal.
      On swaps: no official body publishes one-year CPI inflation swap quotes for free; they come from dealers and data vendors. The Cleveland Fed's 1-year expected inflation is built from inflation swaps, Treasury yields and surveys, which makes it the closest free official stand-in.

      How the math works

      1. Six parts that add up to the whole CPI: shelter, services ex-energy ex-shelter, core goods, food, energy goods, energy services. BLS doesn't publish services ex-shelter as its own index, so it's derived each month as the part of headline CPI the other five don't explain.
      2. Forward monthly changes come from the dials: energy goods = pass-through × monthly oil % change, with oil following actual daily WTI up to today and then moving smoothly to your 12-month target; shelter = its last-3-month pace fading toward new-lease rent growth with the half-life you set; every other part runs at its dial pace.
      3. Headline monthly change = weighted sum of the parts. The weights shift each month as relative prices change, the same way BLS relative importance moves through a year.
      4. Turn it into the reported number: chain the seasonally adjusted index forward, multiply by each calendar month's seasonal factor (the latest observed ratio of the unadjusted to the adjusted index), then take the 12-month change. That gives the figure BLS publishes.
      5. The first one or two months can be pinned to the Cleveland Fed nowcast, which tracks daily gasoline prices. The gap between the nowcast and the model is spread evenly across the parts so they still add up. The track record decides whether this is on by default.
      6. The error band is measured, not assumed. Every month since 2016, the model is re-run using only the data published by that CPI release date (from FRED's ALFRED vintage archive), with the dials set by the same data rule the page uses. The band shows where 80% of those real errors fell at each horizon.
      7. Base effects are what's left over: the 12-month rate depends as much on the month leaving the comparison as on the month coming in. Twelve months out, every current month has left, so the model's answer there is entirely your dials, which is why the outside forecasts above matter.
